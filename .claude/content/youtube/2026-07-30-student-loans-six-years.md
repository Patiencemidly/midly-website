# Six years, six rule changes, and a letter giving you 90 days
**Date:** 2026-07-30 | **Bucket:** Money rules | **Runtime:** ~14 min | **Words:** 2,090

**The position:** Every single change to federal student loans since 2020 was legal, and the combined result still wrecked millions of people's credit. That is not a story about irresponsible borrowers. It is a story about a system nobody was responsible for making coherent, and the bill for it lands on you in a 90-day window most people will not open.

---

## COLD OPEN

For three and a half years, the federal government told forty three million people not to pay their student loans.

Then it told them to start again. Then the Supreme Court said the forgiveness they had been promised was never legal. Then a federal court blocked the repayment plan eight million of them had already enrolled in. Then the government settled that case by killing the plan outright. Then Congress replaced it with something new that went live four weeks ago.

And some time between this month and next March, you are going to get a letter. It gives you ninety days to pick a new plan. If you do not open it, a decision gets made for you, and it is probably the wrong one.

I want to walk through how we actually got here. Because the choice in front of you does not make any sense until you do.

## WHAT HAPPENED

March 2020. The Department of Education suspends payments, sets interest to zero, and stops collections on defaulted loans. It is supposed to be temporary.

It gets extended eight times. Across two administrations, both parties. By the end, the pause has run three and a half years and costs roughly five billion dollars a month.

June 2023. Congress passes the Fiscal Responsibility Act, which bans any further extension. Days later, on June 30th, the Supreme Court decides Biden versus Nebraska. Six to three. The Court holds that the HEROES Act let the Secretary of Education waive or modify provisions in an emergency, but did not authorize cancelling roughly four hundred thirty billion dollars in principal. The ten and twenty thousand dollar forgiveness, which forty three million people had been told was coming and which would have zeroed out the balance of nearly half of them, is gone.

Interest restarts September 2023. Payments restart October 2023. There is a twelve month on-ramp, through September 2024, where missed payments do not count as delinquent.

Meanwhile the administration launches SAVE, a new income-driven plan with much lower payments. Eight million people enroll.

Spring 2024, a federal court enjoins it. July 2024, the Eighth Circuit blocks it entirely. Everyone in SAVE gets dropped into an interest-free forbearance, which means their payments stop counting toward anything. February 2025, the Eighth Circuit rules for the states and tells the lower court to make the injunction broader. April 2025, the broader injunction lands. August 1st, 2025, interest starts accruing again on eight million people who were told to sit still.

December 9th, 2025. The Department settles with Missouri and the other states. SAVE is officially dead.

And on July 1st of this year, RAP goes live. New plan, new rules. The letters start going out.

That is six years. Six changes. Now the part that matters.

## THE DOCUMENT

Here is what you are actually choosing between, and what each one does to you.

**RAP.** The Repayment Assistance Plan. Your payment is a percentage of your total adjusted gross income, not your discretionary income. It starts around one percent at twenty thousand dollars of income and climbs to ten percent above a hundred thousand. Minimum ten dollars a month. You subtract fifty dollars per dependent. Any interest you do not cover gets waived, so the balance stops growing, and there is a fifty dollar a month match against principal. Forgiveness at thirty years.

**IBR, the newer version,** if you first borrowed on or after July 1st, 2014. Ten percent of your discretionary income, and discretionary means your income minus a hundred and fifty percent of the federal poverty line for your household size. Forgiveness at twenty years. No interest subsidy. Your balance can grow.

**IBR, the older version,** if you borrowed before July 2014. Fifteen percent of discretionary income. Twenty five years.

**Standard, or the new Tiered Standard.** A fixed payment. No forgiveness, ever. This is what you get by default if you never respond to the letter.

PAYE and ICR technically still exist, but they are being phased out and everyone has to be off them by July 1st, 2028. Choosing one now is choosing a plan with an expiration date on it.

So which one wins. It depends entirely on your income, and the crossover is not intuitive.

At low income, IBR usually wins, and this surprises people. Because IBR subtracts the poverty line before it takes its ten percent, IBR can put you at zero dollars a month. RAP takes a percentage of every dollar you make, so RAP has a floor of ten dollars. Zero beats ten.

In the middle, roughly forty to seventy thousand dollars for a single borrower, RAP usually wins.

At higher income, IBR wins again, because ten percent of your income minus the poverty line will always be less than ten percent of your whole income.

And now the part that should make you slow down. Moving from IBR to RAP is a one-way door. Your IBR payment history carries into RAP. RAP payments do not carry back into IBR. If you choose RAP and you were wrong, there is no undo.

## THE PART NOBODY IS SAYING

Two things, and I have not heard either one said clearly.

The first is the tax.

The provision in the American Rescue Plan that made income-driven forgiveness tax-free expired on December 31st, 2025. So as of this year, when you finish twenty or thirty years of payments and your balance is forgiven, that forgiven balance counts as income. On an average forgiven balance around forty nine thousand dollars, that is a federal tax bill somewhere between fifty eight hundred and over ten thousand dollars, and your state may want its own cut.

Think about what that does to the twenty versus thirty year question. RAP's longer clock does not just mean ten more years of payments. It means ten more years of balance, and a bigger number sitting on your tax return at the end of it. RAP's interest waiver cuts the other way and helps. You have to actually run it.

And there is one enormous exception. Public Service Loan Forgiveness is still tax-free, and both RAP and IBR count toward it. So the single biggest fork in this entire decision is whether you work for a qualifying employer. If you do, the math is a completely different math. Almost nobody is being told that plainly.

The second thing is bigger.

As of April 2025, thirty one percent of federal student loan borrowers with a payment due were ninety days or more past due. Roughly a million people defaulted in the last quarter of 2025. Two point six million more defaulted in the first quarter of this year. Credit scores for defaulted borrowers fell ninety one points, from an average of 567 to 476. Some projections put as many as thirteen million people in default by the end of this year.

Now. When one in three people subject to a rule fail to comply with it, that is not one in three people being irresponsible. That is a broken rule. If a private lender reported a thirty one percent delinquency rate, nobody would conclude that their customers had a character problem. Everyone would conclude the lender had an operations problem.

But here is the part that actually keeps me up.

Every single one of those changes was legal.

The pause was legal. Ending the pause was legal. Biden versus Nebraska was a legitimate reading of the statute. The injunction against SAVE was legal. The settlement that killed it was legal. Passing RAP was legal. There is no villain here who broke a law. There is no one to sue.

A system can take ninety one points off your credit score, and cost you a mortgage, and there is no moment where anybody did anything wrong. Because nobody's job was to make the whole thing make sense to the person on the other end of it. Everyone was in their lane. The lanes did not connect.

That is the thing I want you to take from this. Not that somebody did this to you on purpose. Something worse. Nobody did.

## THE STRONGEST VERSION OF THE OTHER SIDE

Let me give the other argument its full weight, because it is stronger than my side usually admits.

The pause was indefensible by the end. Five billion dollars a month, extended eight times, long past any honest emergency, by two administrations that both found it politically impossible to be the one to turn it off. That is not compassion. That is cowardice with a deadline that kept moving.

And Biden versus Nebraska was, I think, correctly decided. The HEROES Act was written for national emergencies and it says waive or modify. Cancelling four hundred thirty billion dollars of principal is not modifying. If you want mass forgiveness, Congress has to pass it. The reason Congress never passed it is that Congress never had the votes. Blaming the Court for that is blaming the referee for the score.

There is also a fairness argument that gets waved away too fast. People who paid their loans off, often painfully, were being asked to underwrite people who had not. More than half of American adults never went to college at all, and they were being asked to underwrite it too. That is not resentment and it is not stupidity. It is a real objection, and the refusal to engage with it seriously is a large part of why that side keeps losing.

## WHERE I LAND

The Court was right and the outcome was a disaster, and both of those are true at once, because the Court was never the problem.

The problem is that the executive branch tried to do by memo what it could not do by vote. And when the memo did not hold up, forty three million people were left holding a promise nobody had the authority to make in the first place. The cruelty was not the ruling in June of 2023. The cruelty was three years of announcing relief that had no legal foundation to people who were organizing their lives around it, and then withdrawing it one court order at a time.

And here is the part that will annoy the people who usually agree with me. I would not have paused for three and a half years. A year, maybe eighteen months, with a real restart plan and real communication. What we did instead taught a generation that this payment was optional, dismantled the machinery that used to collect it, and then treated the resulting confusion as delinquency and put it on their credit reports.

The lesson is not that nobody should have tried. The lesson is that relief delivered by executive action is relief that can be taken away, and eventually it always is. Anything that matters this much to this many people either gets legislated, or it is not real. It is a press release with a countdown on it.

## WHAT THIS MEANS IF IT'S YOU

Do not wait for your letter. Log into StudentAid.gov today and find out what plan you are actually on right now, because a lot of people genuinely do not know.

Notices are going out from July of this year through March of next year, and your ninety days runs from your notice, not from a national date. The earliest exit deadline is September 29th, 2026.

If you do nothing, you get moved to Standard or Tiered Standard. Fixed payment, no forgiveness. For many people that payment is several times what an income-driven payment would be.

Run your own numbers instead of taking a rule of thumb from anyone, me included. Roughly: low income favors IBR, because the poverty line deduction can get you to zero and RAP cannot go below ten dollars. The middle band favors RAP. Higher income favors IBR again.

Treat the choice as permanent, because in one direction it is. IBR history moves into RAP. RAP does not move back.

Find out today whether your employer qualifies for Public Service Loan Forgiveness. It changes everything, and that forgiveness is still tax-free.

If you are not going for PSLF, plan for the tax at the end. Forgiveness became taxable income again on January 1st of this year.

And if you are already delinquent or in default, that is a different conversation with its own paths out. Do not let the embarrassment stop you from opening the envelope. It is a form. It is not a verdict.

## CLOSE

Six years. Six changes. Every one of them lawful.

So here is what I actually want to know, and I want the honest version, not the version that makes you look good.

If you had known in March of 2020 exactly how this would end, what would you have done differently?

And then the harder question. Could you actually have done it? Or is the real answer that you were never going to be able to, and the last six years just took a very long time to tell you so.
