# They capped what you can borrow. They didn't cap what it costs.
**Date:** 2026-07-30 | **Bucket:** Money rules | **Runtime:** ~13 min | **Words:** 1,940

**The position:** Capping federal graduate borrowing is defensible and probably overdue, but Congress passed the half that hits students and skipped the half that hits schools, so the debt does not shrink. It moves to private lenders with none of the protections, and a cosigner requirement quietly becomes a wealth test on entry to the professions.

---

## COLD OPEN

Four weeks ago, the federal government stopped lending law students enough money to go to law school.

I want to give you the two numbers before I give you anything else, because the two numbers are the whole story.

Starting July 1st, the most a new law or medical student can borrow from the federal government is fifty thousand dollars a year. Three years of law school, that is a hundred and fifty thousand dollars, and that is the ceiling.

The average total cost of attendance at a private nonprofit law school, for a student graduating this year, was two hundred thirty six thousand dollars.

So there is a gap of about eighty six thousand dollars. It did not disappear. Somebody is going to lend it. Let's talk about who.

## WHAT HAPPENED

The change comes out of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which was signed in July of 2025. The student loan provisions were written to take effect a year later, which is why almost nobody was talking about this when it passed and everybody is talking about it now. It went live July 1st, 2026.

Here is what actually changed.

Grad PLUS is gone. Grad PLUS was the federal program that let a graduate student borrow up to the full cost of attendance, whatever the school said that number was. No new borrowers as of July 1st.

In its place there are hard caps, and they are tiered. If you are in a professional program, which means law, medicine, dentistry, a handful of others, you can borrow fifty thousand dollars a year, with a lifetime limit of two hundred thousand. If you are in a regular master's or doctoral program, it is twenty thousand five hundred a year, with a lifetime limit of one hundred thousand. And across everything you have ever borrowed federally, undergrad included, there is now a total lifetime ceiling of two hundred fifty seven thousand five hundred dollars.

If you already had a federal Direct Loan for the program you are currently in, before July 1st, you are grandfathered. You can keep borrowing under the old rules for up to three more years to finish.

Repayment changed too. There is a new income-driven plan called RAP, the Repayment Assistance Plan, and for new federal borrowers it is now the only income-driven option. Payments run from ten dollars a month at the bottom up to ten percent of your adjusted gross income, minus fifty dollars per dependent, and anything left is forgiven at thirty years. PAYE and ICR are being phased out. If you are on one of them, you have to move by July 1st, 2028.

That is the change. Now the part that requires reading two documents next to each other.

## THE DOCUMENT

Let's do the arithmetic out loud, because the arithmetic is where this stops being an abstraction.

Fifty thousand a year, three years of law school. A hundred and fifty thousand dollars in federal money. Notice that the two hundred thousand dollar lifetime cap is not even the thing biting you. You cannot reach it in three years. The annual number is the real constraint.

Now the cost side. The average total cost of attendance at a private nonprofit law school was eighty three thousand seven hundred and thirty dollars for the 2025 to 2026 academic year. Cost of attendance is not just tuition. It is the number the school itself certifies as what it takes to be a student there. Tuition, fees, housing, books.

Across three years, that came to two hundred thirty six thousand dollars for the class graduating this year.

Two hundred thirty six thousand minus a hundred and fifty thousand is eighty six thousand dollars. That is the hole at the average private law school. At a top school in an expensive city, where three-year cost of attendance runs north of three hundred thirty thousand, the hole is closer to a hundred and eighty thousand.

And here is the part I want you to sit with, because it cuts the other way and it matters. At the cheapest public law schools, where three years of cost of attendance runs about a hundred and fifty five thousand dollars, the new federal cap covers it. Almost exactly. The cap is not a problem at a public school. The cap is a problem at a school charging two and a half times what a public school charges.

Which tells you something. The cap did not create the gap. The price created the gap. The cap just made the price visible.

## THE PART NOBODY IS SAYING

Here is what I have not heard anybody say clearly in four weeks of coverage.

This policy does not reduce anyone's debt. It changes who holds it, and it strips the protections off it on the way out the door.

The eighty six thousand dollars does not evaporate because Congress stopped lending it. The student still has to pay the school. So the student goes to a private lender. And a private student loan is a fundamentally different product from a federal one.

There is no RAP. There is no ten dollar a month payment when you are earning nothing. There is no percentage of income, so if your income drops, your payment does not. There is no forgiveness at thirty years, or at any year. There is no guaranteed discharge if you become permanently disabled. There is no guaranteed discharge if you die, which means in a lot of cases somebody else is holding that note. And it is still extraordinarily hard to discharge in bankruptcy.

And there is one more thing about private student loans that I think is the actual story here, and it is so ordinary that it slides right past people.

You need a cosigner.

A private lender is underwriting a twenty four year old with no income. They will not do it on the student's credit, because the student does not have any. They need somebody with income and a credit score to sign the note.

So think about what the admissions requirements for the legal profession now functionally are. LSAT. GPA. Personal statement. And a family member with good credit willing to put their name on eighty six thousand dollars.

Nobody wrote that last one down. Nobody voted on it. It is not in the bill. It is just what happens when you cap the lender of last resort and leave the price where it is. That is a wealth test on entry to the professions, administered by a credit bureau, and it lands hardest on exactly the students who were the first in their family to get anywhere near this door.

That is not a side effect. That is the mechanism.

## THE STRONGEST VERSION OF THE OTHER SIDE

Now let me give you the argument for this, and I want to give it to you honestly, because it is not stupid and I actually think it is half right.

Grad PLUS was a blank check. The federal government agreed to lend whatever a school certified as the cost of attendance. The school set the number. The government paid the number. If you design a system where the lender will fund any price and the seller sets the price, you know exactly what happens next, and for twenty years that is exactly what happened. Graduate tuition did not rise because the education got better. It rose because the ceiling came off.

And the damage was not evenly spread either. There are master's programs in this country that cost a hundred thousand dollars and lead to jobs paying forty five. Grad PLUS funded every single one of them, in full, no questions asked, no outcome data required. Real people are carrying that debt right now.

So if you are a member of Congress and you want to do something about graduate school prices, you do not actually have a lever on tuition. You cannot set what a private university charges. The only lever you genuinely control is how much you are willing to lend. Pulling that lever is not cruelty. It is the one thing in reach.

And I will give the bill this too. The same law expanded Pell Grants to short-term workforce programs. Nursing assistant training, early childhood education, automotive. Maximum award of seven thousand three hundred ninety five dollars for 2026 to 2027. That is money going to people that higher education funding has ignored for forty years, and it is good, and I am not going to pretend otherwise just because I do not like the rest of the bill.

## WHERE I LAND

I think the cap is right. And I think it does not do what its supporters say it does, because they only passed half of it.

If you cap what a student can borrow and you do not touch what a school can charge, you have not lowered the price of anything. You have moved the financing to a worse lender and added a credit check to the door. The student pays the same two hundred thirty six thousand dollars. They just pay it to somebody who is not required to care what happens to them.

The honest version of this policy pairs the borrowing cap with something on the institutional side. Accreditation tied to what graduates actually earn. Required price disclosure with real outcome data, not the marketing version. Borrowing limits that scale with a program's employment numbers, so the schools placing their graduates keep access and the ones selling a hundred thousand dollar credential into a forty five thousand dollar market do not.

Congress did the half that hits twenty four year olds. It skipped the half that hits institutions with lobbyists and endowments and alumni in the building.

And the thing that should bother all of us is that the argument on TV is not about any of this. One side is calling it fiscal responsibility. The other side is calling it an attack on education. They are having the same fight they have every week, with the same scripts, and neither of them is talking about the price.

The price is the entire story. Everything else is noise.

## WHAT THIS MEANS IF IT'S YOU

If you already had a federal Direct Loan for your current program before July 1st, you are grandfathered for up to three more years. Get that confirmed by your financial aid office in writing. Do not assume it, and do not take it from a phone call.

If you are starting a program this fall, you are under the new caps, and you should know your number before orientation, not after.

Before you sign a private loan, price what you are giving up, not just the interest rate. Ten dollar minimum payments. Percentage of income instead of a fixed bill. Forgiveness at thirty years. A private loan has none of those and the rate is not the reason.

If you are on PAYE or ICR right now, you have to move to another plan by July 1st, 2028. Put it in your calendar today, because two years is exactly long enough to forget.

And if you are choosing between schools, the gap between a hundred and fifty five thousand dollar public school and a two hundred thirty six thousand dollar private one is no longer cushioned by anything. That was always a real difference. It is just no longer hidden.

## CLOSE

I want to leave you with the question I cannot stop turning over.

You get one lever. You can cap what a student is allowed to borrow, or you can cap what a school is allowed to charge. Not both. One.

Because the honest answer is that most people saying they want education to be affordable have only ever been willing to pull the first one. It is easier. It polls better. It does not require telling a university anything.

So which one would you pull. And be honest about who eats the cost of your answer.
